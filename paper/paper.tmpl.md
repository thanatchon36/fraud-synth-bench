# Introduction

Transaction fraud detection is a well-established application of machine learning, yet its empirical literature is hard to build on. Most strong claims rest on proprietary data from a bank or platform [@dalpozzolo2018realistic; @branco2020interleaved; @rao2022xfraud; @krutikov2024booking], and the one widely used public card dataset is anonymised by PCA, which leaves no account, device or merchant structure to study. Three practical questions are therefore poorly answered. Does the recent wave of tabular deep learning and tabular foundation models help in the extreme-imbalance regime where fraud lives [@gorishniy2021ftt; @hollmann2025tabpfnv2]? Do graph neural networks earn their complexity over gradient-boosted trees given good relational features [@weber2019elliptic; @tang2023gadbench]? And how do the operational facts of fraud, namely adversarial drift, label delay and limited investigator capacity [@dalpozzolo2018realistic; @gama2014drift], change the ranking of model families?

We answer these questions on **synthetic data whose fraud mechanisms are known and controllable**. Our contributions are:

1. **`fraudlab.synth`**, a seeded, parameterised generator with eight pluggable fraud scenarios (card testing, account takeover, velocity abuse, friendly fraud, bust-out, mule networks, merchant collusion and synthetic identities), drift schedules, a label-delay model, and a typed account–merchant–device graph. The output is content-hashed and bitwise reproducible.
2. **Five pre-registered research questions** whose hypotheses, refutation criteria and numeric thresholds were fixed before any data was generated (repository file `docs/RESEARCH_QUESTIONS.md`).
3. **A leakage-tested evaluation harness**: strict temporal splits, past-only features verified by future-truncation tests, a time-respecting GraphSAGE sampler, five paired seeds per comparison with Holm correction, and an equivalence margin.
4. **Negative results stated plainly.** The modern tabular transformer did not beat a tuned gradient-boosted tree at any fraud rate. GraphSAGE did not beat a tree ensemble given hand-crafted graph features. Adding anomaly scores did not offset label delay. Validation-holdout retraining did not recover from drift at all.

# Related work

**Classical learners and practitioner evaluation.** Random forests and gradient boosting on engineered aggregation features are the long-standing industrial baseline [@dalpozzolo2014lessons; @bahnsen2016feature]. Dal Pozzolo et al. formalised the operating conditions of a fraud-detection system: an alert budget, investigator feedback and delayed labels. They argued for top-$k$ alert precision over AUC [@dalpozzolo2018realistic], and showed that undersampling distorts probabilities in a correctable way [@dalpozzolo2015calibrating]. Cost-sensitive learning frames the decision as thresholding calibrated probabilities [@elkan2001cost; @bahnsen2015costtrees]. Independent tabular benchmarks consistently find tree ensembles hard to beat [@grinsztajn2022trees; @shwartzziv2022tabular], but these benchmarks deliberately exclude severe imbalance and temporal splits.

**Unsupervised and semi-supervised detection.** Isolation Forest [@liu2008iforest], one-class methods [@scholkopf2001ocsvm] and autoencoders [@pang2021deepad] don't need labels. ADBench reports that even about 1% labelled anomalies beat the best unsupervised detector [@han2022adbench]. Adding outlier scores as features to a supervised model gives modest gains on real card data [@carcillo2021combining].

**Sequence models.** LSTMs and GRUs over a card's history complement engineered features [@jurgovsky2018sequence], and feature-free interleaved RNNs are reported to beat a production tree system [@branco2020interleaved]. Transformers over transaction sequences continue this line [@padhi2021tabformer; @aminian2025fraudtransformer]. All of these results use proprietary data or unequal feature sets.

**Graph methods.** GNNs designed for fraud handle camouflage [@dou2020caregnn] and class imbalance [@liu2021pcgnn], and heterogeneous transaction graphs have been deployed at scale [@rao2022xfraud; @lu2022bright]. On Elliptic, however, a random forest on node features beat a GCN [@weber2019elliptic], and GADBench finds tree ensembles with neighbourhood aggregation competitive with or better than specialised GNNs [@tang2023gadbench]. No study varies how much relational structure the fraud actually has.

**Foundation-model era.** FT-Transformer is the strongest of the simple deep tabular baselines [@gorishniy2021ftt], and SAINT adds row attention and pretraining [@somepalli2021saint]. TabPFN performs in-context prediction on up to 10,000 rows [@hollmann2023tabpfn; @hollmann2025tabpfnv2]. At Booking.com, transformers pretrained on large volumes of traffic beat tuned GBDTs under selection bias [@krutikov2024booking]. LLMs can engineer features [@hollmann2023caafe] but lag specialised classifiers when used directly on tabular fraud data [@hegselmann2023tabllm; @tan2026finfre].

**Synthetic fraud data.** PaySim simulates mobile money with one main fraud pattern [@lopezrojas2016paysim]. AMLworld generates laundering typologies with complete labels [@altman2023amlworld]. BAF provides GAN-generated account-opening data with bias variants [@jesus2022baf], and the ULB handbook ships a simple three-scenario simulator with a delay-aware protocol [@leborgne2022handbook]. Generic tabular generators [@xu2019ctgan] need real data to fit. Our generator combines pluggable multi-scenario fraud, drift schedules, label delay and a device graph in one seeded library.

# Synthetic data framework

**World model.** Accounts have heterogeneous activity (Gamma-distributed rates), log-normal amounts with account- and category-level locations, von Mises time-of-day preferences, five habitual merchants (65% of card payments) and three P2P contacts. Card-present payments carry no customer device, while online payments and transfers do. A single `noise` knob scales legitimate look-alikes of fraud signals: trips abroad, devices shared within a household, phone switches, one-off devices and unusually large purchases. It was set to {{ data.small.noise | .1f }} by a pre-registered difficulty check that looked only at validation data (repository `docs/DECISIONS.md`, D014).

**Fraud scenarios.** Each scenario is a class that emits the transactions of one fraud event. The generator repeats events until a per-segment budget (fraud rate × legitimate volume) is met, and trims the time-ordered tail of the last event so realised rates are exact. Three scenarios are *ring* scenarios defined by relational structure: mule networks (victims → young mule accounts on shared devices → a collector), colluding merchants (a new merchant billing stolen cards) and synthetic identities (fresh accounts sharing devices). Friendly fraud is the cardholder's own normal-looking purchase, later disputed. It is close to undetectable by design and caps achievable recall.

**Labels.** Each fraud event is detected with probability 0.95. Its label arrives after a delay drawn from Uniform(7, 30) days, plus jitter, and 1.5× longer for friendly fraud. Undetected fraud stays labelled legitimate forever.

**Presets.** The main experiments use `small`: {{ data.small.n_transactions | int }} transactions, {{ data.small.n_accounts | int }} accounts, {{ data.small.n_merchants | int }} merchants and {{ data.small.n_devices | int }} devices over {{ data.small.n_days | int }} days, at a {{ data.small.fraud_rate | pct1 }} fraud rate for seed 0. The time-dependent RQs use `small_long`: {{ data.small_long.n_transactions | int }} transactions over {{ data.small_long.n_days | int }} days, the same row budget with a longer timeline. Every dataset is regenerated from its seed inside the experiment, and its content hash is logged with each run.

# Experimental setup

**Features.** Each transaction gets {{ n_tabular | int }} past-only *tabular* features: amount, category, channel, time of day, account and merchant age, velocity counts and sums over 1 h, 24 h and 7 d, amount relative to the account's 30-day mean, device novelty, transfers received, and known prior fraud on the account. It also gets {{ n_graph | int }} *graph* features: accounts per device, counterparty fan-in and sender count, merchant customer base, known-fraud counts on the device, merchant and counterparty, and the account's connected-component size and PageRank in a start-of-day snapshot. A feature for a transaction at time $T$ uses only earlier transactions and labels available before $\min(T, \text{deployment})$. A test checks that features are unchanged when all data after an arbitrary time is deleted.

**Models.** Logistic regression (C chosen on validation), random forest (300 trees), LightGBM with library defaults, and LightGBM tuned by Optuna TPE on validation AUPRC with early stopping (30 trials in RQ1, 15 in RQ3/RQ4, equal across compared arms). Isolation Forest and an MLP autoencoder are unsupervised references. The GRU encodes an account's previous 16 transactions together with the current row's features. FT-Transformer [@gorishniy2021ftt] uses $d=64$, 3 blocks and 8 heads. GraphSAGE uses two mean-aggregator layers over a time-respecting, uniformly sampled neighbourhood (fan-out 10, 5) of the transaction's account, merchant or counterparty, and device. The three neural models train on all positives plus a fresh 10% negative sample per epoch, up to 20 epochs with early stopping on validation AUPRC. Their probabilities are corrected for undersampling [@dalpozzolo2015calibrating]. TabPFN [@hollmann2025tabpfnv2] is implemented (CPU, a stratified temporal subsample of at most 10,000 training rows), but its weights require a license token. It was skipped in the reported run (`results/skipped.json`: {{ skipped.rq1/tabpfn | str }}).

**Protocol.** Splits are strictly temporal: 60/15/25% of the timeline for train, validation and test. The model is frozen at the start of the test window ("deployment"). Unless stated otherwise, training and validation labels are those of detected fraud ("oracle"), and the evaluation target is ground truth, including undetected fraud. Each seed regenerates the dataset and re-seeds every model, and all models share the dataset within a seed, so comparisons are paired. The primary metric is test AUPRC. Secondary metrics are ROC-AUC, daily precision@$k$ and recall@$k$ ($k = {{ rq5.cost_model.alerts_per_day | int }}$ alerts/day), F1 at the validation-optimal threshold, ECE, and latency.

**Statistics.** For each pre-registered comparison we report the mean paired difference over 5 seeds, its 95% $t$-interval, and a Holm-adjusted paired $t$-test within each RQ. "A beats B" requires adjusted $p<0.05$ *and* a mean difference of at least $\delta = 0.02$ AUPRC. "Equivalent" requires the 95% CI to lie within $\pm\delta$. Everything else is *inconclusive*.

# Threats to validity

**Synthetic is not real.** This is the principal threat. Our scenarios are clean mechanisms with stylised parameters. Real fraud mixes tactics, adapts to the deployed model, and lives in far richer feature spaces. Absolute AUPRC values are not comparable with any published real-data result, and we make no claim that a model ranking here transfers to a given institution. What does transfer is the *conditional* evidence: under mechanism X, model family Y behaves like Z.

**Construct validity.** Friendly fraud is near-undetectable by construction, which caps every model's recall. The ring scenarios produce few events per dataset (DATA_CARD limitation 3), so RQ3 has low power. Label delay is modelled as independent of model decisions, so there is no investigator feedback loop (FUTURE_WORK).

**Internal validity.** Hyperparameter budgets are modest and unequal across families. LightGBM gets Optuna; the neural models get fixed, literature-default architectures with early stopping. A larger neural budget might change RQ1. Thresholds were pre-registered, but some protocol choices were made after early infrastructure runs and are documented with their reasons, including one in which two test values were visible (DECISIONS D024–D028).

**Statistical conclusion validity.** Five seeds give wide intervals, and the three-way verdict deliberately reports "inconclusive" rather than over-reading them.

# Conclusion

On controlled synthetic fraud data, the classic toolkit held up. A tuned gradient-boosted tree, given good past-only features and, where relevant, hand-crafted graph features, matched or beat a tabular transformer, a sequence model and a graph neural network in every pre-registered comparison where a difference could be resolved. The modern models did not help here, and in some regimes they cost more compute for less accuracy. This is not a claim that they never help: TabPFN could not be evaluated, the neural models received fixed rather than searched architectures, and the data are synthetic. The operational questions mattered more than the choice of model family. A change in fraud tactics wiped out {{ rq2.degradation.lgbm_tuned.degradation.mean | pct0 }} or more of every supervised model's ranking quality, a realistic label delay removed {{ rq4.relative_loss.lgbm_tuned.mean | pct0 }} of the best model's, and the speed of recovery depended more on the retraining pipeline (refit on everything vs. hold out a validation window) than on the model. For practitioners this suggests three priorities: invest in retraining cadence and label latency, rank alerts by expected loss rather than by probability, and judge models on recovered value under capacity, because AUPRC ranks them only approximately. The generator, harness and every number here are reproducible with `make reproduce`, and we invite extensions through new scenarios and models.
